What the exam actually is

Retaker strategy

What is different the second time

You already own the content once. The failure mode for a retaker is not ignorance — it is recognition without recall: the material feels familiar when you read it, so you never test whether you can produce it cold. Reading the chapter again reinforces the illusion.

Method

Answer questions before reviewing the section. Miss it, then read. The failed retrieval attempt is what makes the reading stick — and it tells you which of the seven domains is actually costing you the section.

Highest-yield fix

If you failed Section 2: drill Exercise Technique and Program Design only — together they are 71% of that section. Testing and Org/Admin can be salvaged in a weekend; technique cannot.

Highest-yield fix

If you failed Section 1: Exercise Science is 55%, and inside it bioenergetics, endocrine response, and neuromuscular adaptation carry most of the items. Sport Psychology at 24% is the cheapest section on the whole exam to raise — it is definitions, and it is 19 items.

Distinction that gets tested

Fast vs. slow glycolysis is decided by the fate of pyruvate, not by speed of movement. Pyruvate → lactate = fast glycolysis. Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA into the Krebs cycle = slow (oxidative) glycolysis. Lactate is not a waste product; it is shuttled and reconverted to glucose in the liver via the Cori cycle.

Neuromuscular essentials

  • Motor unit = one alpha motor neuron + all fibers it innervates. All fibers in a unit are the same type; the unit fires all-or-none. Force is graded by recruitment and by rate coding (firing frequency).
  • Size principle: motor units are recruited from smallest (Type I) to largest (Type IIx) as force demand rises. The practical consequence — to train high-threshold units you need high force or high velocity intent, not just fatigue.
  • Muscle spindle: parallel to fibers, senses rate and magnitude of stretch, triggers the stretch reflex (excitatory). Golgi tendon organ: in series at the musculotendinous junction, senses tension, causes autogenic inhibition (inhibitory).
  • Stretch-shortening cycle: eccentric (energy stored in series elastic component, spindles stimulated) → amortization (the pause; shorter = more power, this is the trainable link) → concentric (stored energy + reflex contribution released).
  • Excitation–contraction coupling: action potential → T-tubule → SR releases Ca²⁺ → Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin shifts → cross-bridge → ATP hydrolysis powers the power stroke; ATP is also required to detach the cross-bridge (why rigor occurs without it).

Bone, connective tissue, and the specificity of loading

  • Wolff's law / minimal essential strain (MES): bone remodels in response to loads above a threshold. Below MES, no osteogenic stimulus. New bone forms at the region of the strain, so the exercise must load the target site.
  • Osteoblasts build, osteoclasts resorb. Bone modeling responds to magnitude, rate, and direction of load — structural exercises that load the axial skeleton (squat, deadlift, power clean) are the strongest stimulus.
  • Tendon, ligament, and cartilage adapt more slowly than muscle. This is the physiological reason for the conservative progression rules you will see again in Program Design.

Sequence to know

Overreaching (short-term, planned, supercompensates after rest) → nonfunctional overreaching (weeks to months to recover) → overtraining syndrome (months+, requires medical management). A planned overreach followed by a taper is a training tool; the same load without the taper is a problem.

Populations and environment

  • Youth: resistance training is safe and effective when supervised and technique-first. The classic injury risk is to the epiphyseal (growth) plate, almost always from unsupervised maximal lifting or poor technique — not from properly programmed training. Preadolescents gain strength primarily through neural adaptation with little hypertrophy.
  • Female athletes: greater Q angle and landing mechanics contribute to elevated non-contact ACL injury rates; neuromuscular and plyometric training reduce that risk. Relative strength differences are largely explained by lean mass and distribution, not by trainability.
  • Older adults: sarcopenia hits Type II fibers first; power declines faster than strength. Train power (moderate load, intentional concentric velocity) as well as strength.
  • Altitude: lower partial pressure of O₂ → ↓ V̇O₂max and endurance performance; anaerobic and sprint performance is largely preserved (and throwing/jumping may improve with less air resistance). Acclimatization takes about two weeks at moderate altitude, with erythropoietin-driven ↑ red cell mass over weeks.
  • Heat: exertional heat illness progresses cramps → heat exhaustion → heat stroke (medical emergency: CNS dysfunction, core temp ≥40 °C/104 °F). Treatment for suspected heat stroke is cool first, transport second — cold-water immersion.
Traps in this domain
  • Hyperplasia vs. hypertrophy: the exam expects hypertrophy (fiber size) as the mechanism in humans; hyperplasia (fiber number) is unproven.
  • Lactate is not the cause of DOMS, and lactate does not cause the burn by itself — H⁺ accumulation does. Lactate clears within about an hour.
  • Neural before structural: early strength gains (first ~4–8 weeks) are predominantly neural; measurable hypertrophy comes later.
  • Concentric–eccentric force: eccentric force capacity exceeds isometric, which exceeds concentric. Force–velocity relationship: as concentric velocity ↑, force ↓.
Test yourself on this domain before you re-read it.

Motivation, goals, confidence

  • Intrinsic vs. extrinsic: intrinsic motivation comes from the activity itself. Extrinsic rewards that are informational (recognizing competence) support intrinsic motivation; rewards that are controlling ("do it or lose your spot") undermine it.
  • Achievement goal theory: a task (mastery) orientation judges success against one's own improvement and is associated with persistence; an ego (outcome) orientation judges success against others and is fragile when losing.
  • Goal setting: specific, measurable, difficult-but-attainable, and written down. Use process and performance goals (under the athlete's control) rather than outcome goals (dependent on opponents). Combine short-term goals that ladder to long-term ones.
  • Self-efficacy — Bandura's four sources, in order of strength: past performance accomplishments → vicarious experience (modeling) → verbal persuasion → physiological/emotional state interpretation.
  • Ideal performance state / flow: absence of self-doubt and fear of failure, narrowed and effortless attention, sense of control, distorted time, no conscious effort. Note it is characterized by low anxiety, not high arousal.

Coaching cue rule

An external attentional focus (the outcome or the implement — "drive the floor away," "explode the bar off the chest") consistently outperforms an internal focus ("contract your quads") for motor performance and learning. Expect at least one item on this.

Traps in this domain
  • Arousal ≠ anxiety. Arousal is physiological activation and is value-neutral. Anxiety is the negative emotional state, with cognitive (worry) and somatic (physical) components. Somatic anxiety peaks at the start of competition and drops; cognitive anxiety can stay elevated throughout.
  • State vs. trait: trait anxiety is the stable personality disposition; state anxiety is right now.
  • The right amount of arousal depends on the task: gross-motor, high-force tasks (deadlift, block) tolerate high arousal; fine-motor, complex tasks (free throw, putt, pitching accuracy) require lower arousal.
  • Injury response follows a grief-like sequence — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance — and the coach's job is social support and involvement, not psychotherapy. Refer when the response is prolonged or severe.

Body composition change

  • Gain: ≈+350–700 kcal/day above maintenance supports about 0.25–0.5 kg (0.5–1 lb) per week of lean gain alongside resistance training. Faster than that is mostly fat.
  • Loss: a deficit of 500–1000 kcal/day yields roughly 0.5–1 kg/week; keep loss at or under about 1% of body mass per week to protect lean tissue, and keep protein at the high end while in deficit.
  • Female athlete triad / RED-S: low energy availability → menstrual dysfunction → low bone mineral density. Low energy availability is the driver, and it does not require disordered eating to occur. Refer to a physician and RD.
Scope of practice — expect an item

A CSCS may provide general nutrition information (public dietary guidelines, hydration, general timing). Assessing nutritional status, prescribing individualized diets, or treating a clinical condition or eating disorder is the registered dietitian's or physician's role. When an item describes an athlete with suspected disordered eating, the correct action is always refer — not counsel, not weigh more often.

Supplement regulation

Under DSHEA (1994), supplements are not approved by the FDA before sale and the burden is on the FDA to prove a product unsafe. Manufacturers must not claim to treat or cure disease. Recommend only third-party-certified products — NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport — because contamination, not the label ingredient, is what causes failed drug tests.

Exercise Technique

The single largest domain on the exam and the home of most video items. You are being asked to watch a lift and name the fault, the cue, or the spotting decision — practice describing errors out loud, not just recognizing correct form.

Breathing rule

Exhale through the sticking point (the hardest part of the concentric), inhale during the eccentric. The Valsalva maneuver raises intra-abdominal pressure and stabilizes the spine, but it also spikes blood pressure — reserve it for experienced athletes on maximal or near-maximal structural lifts, and hold it only for the few seconds through the sticking point. It is contraindicated for anyone with cardiovascular or blood pressure concerns.

Faults and the cue that fixes them

  • Back squat, knees caving (valgus): cue "knees out over the toes," check ankle mobility and stance width, reduce load. This is also the ACL-risk pattern in landing.
  • Back squat, heels rising / excessive forward lean: ankle dorsiflexion limitation or bar too far forward. Do not solve it by putting plates under the heels as a permanent fix.
  • Deadlift, hips rising first: the athlete is not maintaining the back angle; cue "chest up, push the floor away," lower the load.
  • Bench press, bouncing the bar off the chest or lifting the buttocks: both break the five-point contact rule and both mean the load is too heavy.
  • Power clean, arm pull: "arms are straps until the shrug" — the bar is moved by triple extension, not the elbows.
  • Landing in plyometrics, stiff or noisy: cue a quiet landing, hips back, knees tracking, "spend as little time on the ground as possible" only after the landing is sound.

Traps in this domain
  • Belts: appropriate for maximal or near-maximal sets of structural exercises that load the spine. Not for every set, and not for exercises that do not load the trunk.
  • Warm-up order: general (5–10 min low-intensity aerobic to raise tissue temperature) → specific (dynamic, movement-matched). Never static-stretch a cold muscle as the warm-up.
  • Machine vs. free weight: machines fix the movement path and are easier to learn and to spot; free weights load stabilizers and allow sport-specific, multi-planar movement. Neither is universally "better" — the item is about the athlete and the goal.
  • Bar placement: high-bar back squat (on the trapezius above the posterior deltoids) vs. low-bar (across the posterior deltoids/spine of the scapula) changes the torso angle and the hip/knee contribution.

Section 2 · 35% · ≈38 items

Program Design

Items give you an athlete, a season, and a goal, then ask for load, reps, sets, rest, exercise order, or the next block. Every answer traces back to a needs analysis.

The needs analysis — step one, always

  • Movement analysis: body and limb movement patterns, muscular involvement, planes of motion.
  • Physiological analysis: strength, power, hypertrophy, and endurance priorities; which energy systems the sport actually uses.
  • Injury analysis: common sites of injury for the sport and prior injury history for this athlete.
  • Athlete evaluation: training status, testing results, training age, and the season.

Vocabulary

Macrocycle = a full training year (or a full competitive cycle). Mesocycle = several weeks to months, one training emphasis. Microcycle = about a week. Linear periodization moves gradually from high volume/low intensity toward low volume/high intensity; undulating (nonlinear) periodization varies volume and intensity within the week.

Concurrent training, tapering, detraining

  • Interference: high-volume aerobic endurance training can blunt strength and especially power adaptations. Mitigate by separating sessions (ideally by hours or days), keeping endurance volume appropriate, and prioritizing the quality that matters most to the sport. Strength training does not similarly impair endurance performance — it usually improves economy.
  • Sequencing on the same day: put the quality you care about most first, when the athlete is fresh.
  • Tapering: reduce volume substantially while maintaining intensity, typically over 7–14 days before competition. Cutting intensity is what makes a taper fail.
  • Detraining: aerobic markers (V̇O₂max, mitochondrial enzymes) fall within about two weeks of inactivity. Strength is retained longer, but power and short-term endurance decay faster than maximal strength.
Traps in this domain
  • In-season means maintenance. Reduce volume and frequency, keep intensity high enough to maintain — do not run an off-season hypertrophy block during the competitive season.
  • Power exercises before everything. If a stem lists a workout with power cleans placed after squats, that ordering is the error.
  • Specificity beats variety. Exercise selection follows the movement analysis; "more variety" is rarely the correct answer.
  • Rest period is the giveaway. If an item describes 30 s rest and 10 reps, the goal is hypertrophy, not strength — the rest interval alone often identifies the intended answer.

Why this order

Anything that produces fatigue contaminates every test that follows it. If only one testing day is available, this order preserves the validity of the maximal power and strength measures — which are the ones most sensitive to prior fatigue. Allow full recovery (often 2–3 min or more) between maximal trials, and if possible split the battery across days.

Basic statistics you need

  • Mean, median, mode — and know that a skewed distribution (a few very strong athletes) pulls the mean but not the median.
  • Standard deviation — spread around the mean. In a normal distribution, ≈68% falls within ±1 SD, ≈95% within ±2 SD.
  • Percentile rank — the percentage of the group scoring below this athlete. A 90th percentile bench press means 90% of the reference group lifted less.
  • Correlation (r) — strength and direction of a relationship, from −1 to +1. Correlation is not causation, and a validity coefficient is a correlation.
Traps in this domain
  • Reliable but not valid is possible (a consistently mis-calibrated scale); valid but not reliable is not.
  • A test's validity is tied to its purpose and population — a valid test for collegiate football linemen is not automatically valid for adolescent swimmers.
  • Test specificity: pick the test that matches the sport's energy system and movement pattern. A 1.5 mile run is a poor test for a lineman regardless of how well it is administered.
  • Standardize everything — same time of day, same equipment, same instructions, same order, same rest, same tester. Any change invalidates the comparison to previous results.
Cheap points if you memorize the order and the validity types.

Legal and administrative duties

  • Informed consent vs. waiver: informed consent documents that the participant understands the risks and procedures — it does not release liability for negligence. A waiver attempts to release liability, and its enforceability varies by jurisdiction; neither excuses negligent supervision.
  • Pre-participation screening and medical clearance before training; know when to refer to a physician.
  • Emergency Action Plan (EAP): written, posted, rehearsed. Includes personnel roles, emergency equipment location (AED), the exact facility address and entrance for EMS, and communication procedure. All staff maintain current CPR/AED certification.
  • Supervision: the professional must be present, credentialed, and positioned to see the floor. Athlete-to-coach ratio should reflect the athletes' training age and the exercises being performed — novice athletes doing complex lifts need the tightest ratio.
  • Records: maintain training logs, injury reports, maintenance and cleaning logs, and screening forms. Injury reports are completed immediately and retained. Health information is confidential (HIPAA/FERPA considerations).
  • Maintenance and cleaning: daily inspection and cleaning of contact surfaces; scheduled preventive maintenance (cables, belts, upholstery, bar knurling, collars, platform integrity); remove and tag defective equipment immediately rather than posting a note on it.
  • Product liability: use equipment only for its intended purpose, follow manufacturer instructions, keep warning labels intact, and never modify equipment.
Answer pattern to recognize

When an Org/Admin item describes something going wrong, the correct answer is almost always the one that reflects documented policy, direct supervision, and a written plan already in place before the incident — not the reactive fix. "Have the athlete sign a waiver" is rarely the right answer; "remove the equipment from service and document it" usually is.
